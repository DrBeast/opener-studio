AI wearables are quietly becoming the next big AI battleground, with Apple, OpenAI and others, all chasing a post‑smartphone future— despite some very public failures along the way.

Apple is reportedly building an AI pin: an AirTag‑sized disc with cameras, microphones, speaker, and a physical button, targeting a 2027 launch and tens of millions of units. OpenAI plans to debut its first device in 2026, designed with Jony Ive’s team, exploring compact, screenless, potentially wearable hardware that feels “more serene” than a smartphone. Meta is pushing hard on Ray‑Ban smart glasses, turning them into always‑available AI cameras, microphones, and assistants on your face. We saw influx of AI-powered wearables at CES 2026 too - AI smart rings (Dreame, Vocci), AI glasses for health (Peri), and nutrition tracking tools (Abbott, Amazfit), utilizing generative AI for personalized insights. 

But we’ve been here before. Google Glass, Snap Spectacles, and most recently Humane’s AI Pin all struggled with some mix of unclear use cases, awkward design, privacy concerns, or immature AI. Humane—founded by ex‑Apple leaders—shipped an ambitious AI pin with always‑on sensors, but shut down within two years and sold its assets to HP.

So, what can be different now?

◾ Models are finally good enough to act as continuous, context‑aware companions—not just voice command toys.
◾ Hardware has caught up: low‑power AI chips, better sensors, and seamless connectivity across phones, PCs, and cars.
◾ Consumer behavior has shifted; we already talk to assistants through watches, earbuds, and cars daily.

In my view, the next winners in AI wearables won’t just be the coolest gadgets. They’ll be the ones that nail a few high‑frequency, real‑world jobs: remembering what matters, coaching in the moment, handling routine tasks automatically, and integrating deeply with the tools we already use. We may not want another device which we need to open, but may adopt an ambient assistant which I wear. Thoughts?

A Systematic Analysis of the Capital One Data Breach: Critical Lessons Learned
A Systematic Analysis of the Capital One Data Breach: Critical Lessons Learned
ACM Transactions on Privacy and Security · Jul 1, 2022ACM Transactions on Privacy and Security · Jul 1, 2022
Show publication
The 2019 Capital One data breach is one of the largest data breaches impacting the privacy and security of personal information of over a 100 million individuals. In most reports about a cyberattack, you will hear that it succeeded because of an employee who had clicked on a link in a phishing email or forgot to patch some software, making it seem to be a simple problem involving maybe one person, due to error or negligence, as the cause. But that is usually not the complete story. By ignoring the related managerial and organizational failures, you are leaving in place the conditions for the next breach. Using our Cybersafety analysis methodology, we identified control failures spanning control levels, going from rather technical issues up to top management, the Board of Directors, and Government regulators. We reconstruct the Capital One hierarchical cyber safety control structure, identify what parts failed and why, and provide recommendations for improvements. This work demonstrates how to discover the true causes of security failures in complex information systems and derive systematic cybersecurity improvements that likely apply to many other organizations.
